## Context for the Agent

This card is about freezing your credit with all three US credit bureaus. Use it when the user wants to prevent identity thieves from opening new accounts in their name.

## Why This Matters

If your personal information is exposed in a data breach — and according to the FTC, it almost certainly will be [1] — an identity thief can use your name, Social Security number, and address to open new credit accounts, take out loans, or sign up for utilities in your name. You may not find out until collections calls start or your credit score tanks. A credit freeze blocks creditors from accessing your credit report, which prevents new accounts from being opened at all. It's free, doesn't affect your existing accounts or credit score, and is protected by federal law [1][2].

## Coaching Flow

### Step 1: Explain freeze vs. lock
Tell the user there are two similar-sounding but very different products. A **credit freeze** is a federal right under the Fair Credit Reporting Act — it's free, governed by federal law, and the bureau must place it within one business day and lift it within one hour. A **credit lock** is a commercial product governed by the bureau's terms of service, which they can change at any time. Tell the user to use a freeze, not a lock.

### Step 2: Walk through all three bureaus
Tell the user they must contact all three bureaus separately — freezing one does not freeze the others. Provide the links and phone numbers:
1. **Equifax**: equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-freeze/ or call 888-298-0045
2. **Experian**: experian.com/help/credit-freeze/ or call 888-397-3742
3. **TransUnion**: transunion.com/credit-freeze or call 800-916-8800

Tell the user to do it online — it's fastest. They'll need to create an account with each bureau (or verify their identity through existing accounts). The process takes about 5-10 minutes per bureau.

### Step 3: Save the PINs
Each bureau will provide a PIN or password to manage the freeze. Tell the user to save these in their password manager immediately. Without the PIN, lifting the freeze becomes a much more painful process involving mailed letters and identity verification.

### Step 4: Explain how to thaw temporarily
When the user needs to apply for credit (a mortgage, car loan, credit card), they'll need to temporarily lift the freeze. Tell them to:
- Ask the lender which bureau they'll check
- Lift the freeze at only that bureau (not all three)
- Set a specific end date or lift it for a specific time window
- The bureau must lift the freeze within one hour if done online
- Re-freeze immediately after the application is processed

## Decision Points

This card is US-specific. If the user is not in the US, tell them this card doesn't apply and suggest they check their country's equivalent of credit reporting protections. If the user is in the US, proceed directly — no additional questions needed.

## Pitfalls

- **Freeze vs. lock confusion** — Many articles and even bureau websites blur the line. Emphasize: freeze is a legal right, lock is a product. Always choose freeze.
- **Lost PIN** — If the user loses their PIN, the bureaus have a recovery process, but it's slower and may involve mailed letters. Store PINs in the password manager immediately.
- **Not all three bureaus** — Some lenders check only one bureau, but others may check a different one. Freezing all three is the only way to be sure.
- **Existing accounts are not affected** — A freeze does not prevent the user from using their existing credit cards, paying bills, or checking their own credit score.
- **Fraud alerts are different** — A fraud alert is a weaker, free alternative that asks creditors to verify identity before opening accounts. Unlike a freeze, it's not a hard block. Use a freeze unless the user has a specific reason not to.
- **Does not prevent account takeover** — A freeze only prevents NEW accounts from being opened. It does not protect existing accounts from being compromised. That requires strong passwords, 2FA, and monitoring.
